# Upper-secondary chemistry learning case: VSEPR and molecular shape

Status: proposed learning-design and capability study. No implementation is authorized by this document.

## 1. Curriculum anchor

This case targets Uganda's 2025 National Curriculum Development Centre (NCDC) Advanced Secondary Chemistry syllabus:

- Senior Five, Term One.
- Topic 3: Bonding and Structure.
- Sub-topic 3.2: Covalent Bonds and Molecular Structures.
- Sub-topic allocation: 18 periods.

The syllabus learning outcome asks learners to analyse covalent bond formation, including dative covalency, and explain molecular structures by applying VSEPR theory to predict molecular shapes and bond angles. Suggested activities include researching and discussing covalent bonding and VSEPR, predicting and drawing molecular/ionic shapes, and building 3D models. Assessment includes probing learners to justify VSEPR reasoning and evaluating drawn models for accuracy.

This document scopes one focused learning case within that sub-topic. It does not claim to cover all 18 periods or all of Sub-topic 3.2. It does not yet specify a particular school timetable or period duration.

## 2. Learning case

**Working title:** Why is water bent while methane is tetrahedral?

**Central question:** How can electron-pair repulsion around a central atom explain the different three-dimensional molecular shapes of H₂O and CH₄?

Water and methane are deliberately contrasting cases:

| Property | Methane (CH₄) | Water (H₂O) |
|---|---|---|
| Central atom | Carbon | Oxygen |
| Bonding pairs around central atom | 4 | 2 |
| Lone pairs on central atom | 0 | 2 |
| Total electron domains | 4 | 4 |
| Electron-domain arrangement | Tetrahedral | Tetrahedral |
| Molecular shape | Tetrahedral | Bent (V-shaped) |
| Approximate bond angle | 109.5° | 104.5° |

The key conceptual distinction is that electron-domain arrangement and molecular shape are not interchangeable. In water, the two lone pairs occupy electron domains but are not atoms; the molecular shape describes the positions of the nuclei/atoms only. Lone-pair repulsion compresses the H–O–H angle below the ideal tetrahedral angle.

These are standard introductory VSEPR teaching examples. The angles are approximate reference values, not measurements derived from the current SVF model. Any digital model must identify whether its coordinates are idealized teaching geometry or sourced molecular geometry.

## 3. Prerequisite knowledge

Before this case, learners should be able to:

- Identify protons, neutrons and electrons and explain atomic number.
- Interpret electron configurations for the relevant atoms at the level taught in the preceding atomic/electronic-structure sub-topic.
- Identify valence electrons.
- Explain covalent bonding as sharing electron pairs.
- Read simple molecular formulae and Lewis electron-dot structures.

The NCDC programme planner places electron configurations before Bonding and Structure in Senior Five Term One. The teacher should check learner readiness rather than assume every learner has mastered the prerequisite.

## 4. Intended learning outcomes

By the end of this case, a learner should be able to:

1. Draw or interpret the Lewis structures of CH₄ and H₂O, distinguishing shared/bonding pairs from lone pairs.
2. Count electron domains around the central atom and use the VSEPR model to explain their arrangement.
3. Distinguish electron-domain geometry from molecular shape.
4. Predict and draw the tetrahedral shape of methane and bent shape of water.
5. Compare the approximate H–C–H and H–O–H bond angles and explain why the water angle is smaller.
6. Use a rotatable 3D representation to justify why a flat drawing can hide important spatial information.
7. State at least one limitation of a ball-and-stick or electron-domain teaching model.

Evidence of understanding is the learner's explanation linking the Lewis structure, electron-domain count, repulsion, and resulting molecular shape—not merely selecting the correct 3D picture.

## 5. Teaching and exploration sequence

This is a suggested sequence for one focused lesson segment. The teacher should fit it into the school's period structure and surrounding instruction.

### Phase A — Elicit and predict

Present the formulae CH₄ and H₂O and ask learners to sketch their Lewis structures independently.

Prompt:
- How many valence electrons does the central atom contribute?
- How many bonding pairs surround the central atom?
- Are there any lone pairs?
- Before seeing a 3D model, what shape do you predict, and why?

Collect predictions before revealing the models. This makes prior reasoning visible and gives the teacher diagnostic evidence.

### Phase B — Construct the electron-domain picture

Learners identify four electron domains around the central atom in each molecule:

- CH₄: four bonding domains.
- H₂O: two bonding domains and two lone-pair domains.

Explain that VSEPR is a model for predicting arrangements by considering repulsion between electron domains. For this introductory comparison, all four domains are arranged approximately tetrahedrally. Lone pairs occupy space and affect the positions of bonding domains, but are not counted as atoms when naming molecular shape.

Do not present lone-pair domains as literal solid balls, electron trajectories, or sharply bounded physical objects. A visual lobe or region is a schematic representation of an electron domain.

### Phase C — Explore methane

Show the tetrahedral arrangement. Learners should:

1. Rotate the model.
2. Identify the central carbon and four hydrogen atoms.
3. Trace all four C–H bonds.
4. Explain why a flat cross or square drawing would misrepresent the three-dimensional arrangement.
5. Relate the arrangement to the approximate 109.5° H–C–H angle.

Ask learners to justify the shape using the four bonding domains, rather than memorising the word “tetrahedral”.

### Phase D — Explore water

Show the water electron-domain arrangement and molecular shape as distinct but related views. Learners should:

1. Identify the central oxygen and two hydrogen atoms.
2. Identify two bonding domains and two lone-pair domains.
3. Hide or fade the lone-pair domains while keeping them available as inspectable information.
4. Observe that the atom positions form a bent shape, not a tetrahedron of three atoms.
5. Explain why the H–O–H angle is approximately 104.5°, smaller than the ideal tetrahedral angle, in terms of stronger repulsion associated with lone pairs.

The presentation must not imply that hiding a lone-pair glyph removes or changes the underlying electron-domain information.

### Phase E — Compare and explain

Place the two structures side by side. Ask learners to complete a comparison:

- Both central atoms have four electron domains.
- Methane has four bonding domains and no lone pairs.
- Water has two bonding domains and two lone pairs.
- Both have approximately tetrahedral electron-domain arrangements.
- Their molecular shapes differ because molecular shape considers atom positions, and lone pairs affect the arrangement of bonding pairs.

Require a written or spoken explanation that connects these statements into a causal account.

### Phase F — Transfer and assessment

Give learners an unfamiliar simple molecule or ion only after the teacher has established the VSEPR method. The teacher selects an example appropriate to the taught scope and checks that its electron-domain data is represented accurately. Learners independently draw the Lewis structure, count domains, predict geometry, and justify the result.

Do not silently use the current H₂O/CH₄ implementation as a general-purpose molecule builder or claim it supports arbitrary VSEPR prediction.

## 6. Formative assessment

Use the NCDC's observation, conversation, and product approach.

### Observation
- Does the learner distinguish bonding pairs from lone pairs?
- Does the learner correctly manipulate and interpret the 3D model?
- Does the learner participate in comparing and revising predictions?

### Conversation / probing questions
- Why do CH₄ and H₂O both have four electron domains but different molecular shapes?
- Are lone pairs included when naming molecular shape? Why?
- Why is the H–O–H angle smaller than the tetrahedral angle?
- What information is lost in a flat drawing?
- Which parts of this model are chemical information and which are visual conventions?

### Learner product
Assess a labelled Lewis structure, electron-domain count, 3D shape sketch, approximate bond angle, and short justification. Score the reasoning and model accuracy, not artistic quality.

A learner has not demonstrated the central outcome merely by memorising “methane is tetrahedral” and “water is bent”.

## 7. Required visual and interaction behaviour

The following are learning requirements to test, not capabilities claimed to exist in current SVF.

### Molecular structure view
- Selectable atom glyphs resolve to exact `AtomId` and element identity.
- Selectable bonds resolve to exact `BondId`, endpoints, and bond order.
- Atom/bond connectivity is derived from the chemistry model, never inferred from screen-space proximity.
- Camera rotation changes only the view, not the molecule's scientific coordinates.

### VSEPR electron-domain view
- Show electron domains around the central atom, with each domain classified as bonding pair or lone pair.
- Preserve links between a bonding domain, its bond, and the relevant atoms.
- Represent lone pairs as electron-domain concepts, not as atoms or chemical bonds.
- Allow the learner to switch between an electron-domain view and an atom-only molecular-shape view without deleting or mutating semantic data.
- Expose domain count, domain type, central atom, and approximate bond-angle information through inspection.
- Make it visually clear which angle is being measured and which geometric points define it.

### Comparison
- Keep CH₄ and H₂O separate, with independent selection and inspection.
- Provide a consistent camera/view convention so learners can compare spatial arrangements.
- Do not distort either structure to make the comparison more visually dramatic.

## 8. Scientific representation requirements and current gap

The existing E1/E2 chemistry model represents elements, atom instances, bonds, bond order, molecular coordinates, and a derived ball-and-stick view. It does **not** currently represent:

- Lone-pair electron domains.
- Electron-domain identity and classification.
- A VSEPR arrangement or molecular-shape classification.
- A semantic relation between an electron domain and its associated atom/bond.
- Explicitly validated bond-angle measurements or geometry provenance.

Consequently, the current ball-and-stick output alone cannot teach the central distinction between electron-domain arrangement and molecular shape. It can show atom positions and bonds, but it cannot show the two lone pairs on oxygen or explain why they matter.

A follow-on design must decide whether VSEPR concepts belong in a dedicated chemistry model layer or a derived educational/analysis representation. Do not mislabel lone pairs as atoms, invent them from geometry, or overload the existing `Bond` type.

For this case, the canonical teaching geometry should be explicitly marked as idealized/reference geometry. If a numeric angle is displayed, it must be derived from documented coordinates or stored as a separately sourced reference quantity with clear semantics. It must not be presented as an experimentally measured value merely because the model displays it.

## 9. Responsibility boundaries

- **SVF:** scientific identities and relationships for atoms, bonds and (if designed) electron domains; deterministic spatial representations; semantic selection and inspection; explicit geometry/angle provenance.
- **Prismal:** not required for this static VSEPR lesson. Consider it only if a later learning objective genuinely requires dynamic simulation or computed behaviour.
- **Mava:** lesson sequence, prediction prompts, staged reveal, learner tasks, comparison, assessment capture, and teacher-controlled progression. It should not duplicate chemical rules or calculate VSEPR independently of the scientific model.

The first implementation need not involve all three systems. A static, inspectable scientific model may be enough to validate the core concept before lesson-authoring integration.

## 10. Deliberate exclusions

This case does not attempt to teach or implement the entirety of Sub-topic 3.2. In particular, defer:

- Dative covalency.
- Molecular and bond polarity.
- Electronegativity and intermolecular forces.
- Hydrogen bonding and Van der Waals forces.
- General molecular/ionic VSEPR coverage beyond the selected examples.
- Molecular dynamics, force fields, reaction pathways, or energy calculations.
- Electron orbitals or literal electron trajectories.
- Arbitrary molecular authoring.

These are syllabus outcomes or adjacent concepts, but they require their own learning cases and scientific representations.

## 11. Decision gate before implementation

Before coding, review this case against the exact chemistry syllabus and confirm:

1. The prerequisite knowledge and lesson placement are appropriate for the intended Senior Five learners.
2. The teacher's explanation of VSEPR and lone-pair repulsion is scientifically accurate.
3. The learner assessment distinguishes recall from explanation and transfer.
4. The representation can show electron domains without confusing them with atoms or bonds.
5. The source and status of all displayed geometry and angle values are explicit.
6. Each required capability has a clear owner.

The first implementation should enable the learner to explain the CH₄/H₂O contrast correctly. Do not add a broad VSEPR engine or general chemistry editor unless this and further real lesson cases demonstrate that need.
